# Lesson 01 — Case Study: Clavicular (Braden Peters)

> Our specimen for understanding the attention economy. Studied, not endorsed.

## A note on the name
You came in with two names — **"Clevicular"** and **"Brandon Peters."** They're the same person:
- **Clavicular** is the brand name. It plays on the **clavicle** (collarbone), which the "looksmaxxing" world treats as a marker of skeletal attractiveness. The name itself is a thesis statement: *your bones are the brand.*
- **Braden Peters** is the real person. "Brandon Peters" is a near-miss memory of that.

This collapse of two names into one is the first lesson. **A personal brand is a character.** The audience bonds with "Clavicular," a confident transformation guru. The human underneath is a 20-year-old named Braden. The distance between the two is a tool — it lets the creator be larger, more certain, and more *useful as a symbol* than any real person could be.

---

## The rise: how he grew fast

Clavicular is a U.S.-based streamer (primarily on **Kick**) whose content centers on **looksmaxxing** — "maximizing" physical appearance through diet, skincare, grooming, and increasingly extreme interventions. His growth wasn't one viral video; it was a *stack* of attention-grabbing moves:

1. **A sticky niche with a built-in audience.** Looksmaxxing was already a churning subculture of insecure young men. He didn't invent the demand — he positioned himself as its face.
2. **Mainstream-media on-ramps.** He went viral after appearing on Michael Knowles' Daily Wire show making "predictions" based on appearance, and through podcast circuits like *Fresh&Fit* and *Raw Talk*.
3. **Spectacle and controversy as fuel.** Livestreamed stunts (a Cybertruck incident), proximity to polarizing figures (Andrew Tate, Nick Fuentes), and partying-with-controversy clips kept him *algorithmically alive*. Outrage is engagement, and engagement is distribution.
4. **Proof-of-loyalty moments.** He reportedly raised **$35,000+ in a single broadcast** through donations, and was at one point estimated to earn around **$100,000/month**. Numbers like these are both income *and* marketing — they signal a devoted tribe, which attracts more followers (social proof).

> **Lens check (Economic):** None of these steps are about looksmaxxing per se. They're about manufacturing *reach*. The product is almost secondary to the attention engine.

---

## The business model

The monetization is clean and instructive:

- **Free tier (the funnel):** Streams and short clips. Personal, casual, "in-person" energy — not gaming, just *him* talking. This builds the parasocial bond (see Lesson 02). Free content's job is not to inform; its job is to make you feel *known* and *understood*.
- **Paid tier (the conversion):** **"Clavicular's Clan,"** a private community at **~$49-50/month**. Members get "detailed guides that will guaranteed ascend you," photo reviews, and supplement/procedure recommendations.

The genius — and the danger — is the **closed loop**: insecurity drives you to the free content, the free content deepens the insecurity ("your natural face is a problem to be solved"), and the paid tier sells the only relief. As one analysis put it, *"Looking better is never quite enough because the system profits from ensuring that."*

> **Lens check (Ethical):** A normal coach solves a problem and you leave. This model is structurally incentivized to *never fully solve* the problem, because a cured customer is a lost subscriber.

---

## The pain points he monetizes

This is the heart of your original question — he "grew through pain points." Here's the map:

| Surface desire | Deeper fear | Identity he sells |
|---|---|---|
| "I want to look better." | "I'm unattractive and will be alone." | "An *ascended* man who has decoded attraction." |
| "I want to be more confident." | "I don't measure up to other men." | "Someone with secret knowledge most men lack." |
| "I want dating success." | "Modern dating is rigged against me." | "A winner in a game everyone else is losing." |

Research context makes the vulnerability real: a Young Men Research Project figure cited that **60% of surveyed young men feel women have "too many expectations."** Clavicular meets that confusion with **deterministic promises** — the idea that physical perfection *guarantees* romantic success. Determinism is comforting. It converts a messy, scary, social problem into a solvable engineering problem with a price tag.

> **Lens check (Psychological):** He's not selling jawlines. He's selling *certainty* to people drowning in uncertainty. That's why it works, and why it's hard to quit.

---

## The controversy (and why it belongs in the study)

We don't study him as entertainment. The case has a genuinely dark trajectory, and ignoring it would make the lesson dishonest:

- **The pipeline problem.** Reporting (Rolling Stone, BPS, PBS) describes a pattern where looksmaxxing content escalates: it can start as skincare and grooming and drift toward **bonesmashing** (striking the jaw — medically condemned as dangerous and useless), aggressive pharmacology, and surgery. Algorithms reward escalation because extremity holds attention.
- **From insecurity to ideology.** The same reporting describes a slide from self-improvement into **grievance-based, misogynistic framing** — "women as the enemy." Rolling Stone documented coursework with language normalizing sexual coercion. This is the dark end of "pain-point marketing": the pain is deepened and then redirected into resentment.
- **Platform consequences.** His YouTube channels were **terminated more than once** (reported in 2025) for violating platform policies.

> **Lens check (Ethical):** The line we're looking for all course — *serving a need vs. manufacturing one* — is visible here. Helping an insecure kid build genuine confidence is service. Deepening his self-loathing to sell a $49 subscription, then routing his pain into ideology, is the opposite.

---

## What to actually learn from this case

Strip away the specifics and you get transferable principles about attention. These are **descriptive, not prescriptive** — understand them so you can *see* them, and decide for yourself what's ethical to use.

1. **Find pain that already exists; don't fake it.** The strongest brands attach to a real, felt ache. (Ethics lives in what you do *next*.)
2. **Free content builds the bond; paid content sells the relief.** The funnel is emotional before it's transactional.
3. **A character scales better than a person.** Certainty, consistency, and a clear symbol beat nuance for reach.
4. **Controversy is distribution.** This is true whether or not you want it to be; the question is what you're willing to trade for reach.
5. **The ethical fork is the loop.** Do you build a business that *cures* the customer (they graduate and thank you) or one that *requires* their continued pain (they can never leave)? Same mechanics, opposite soul.
