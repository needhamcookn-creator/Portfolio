# The Attention Study

> A self-directed learning channel on the philosophy and psychology of attention, social media, and why people become *attached* to other people online.

This is a living notebook. The goal is not to consume content but to build a real mental model of one question:

**What makes a stranger on a screen feel like someone you know — and how do creators turn that feeling into power, money, and influence?**

---

## Why this matters to you

You came in fascinated by a specific story: a creator who "grew his brand through pain points" and rose to fame fast. That instinct is exactly right. The fastest way to understand attention is to study someone who is *unusually good* at capturing it, then reverse-engineer the machinery underneath.

One thing to clear up first, because it reframes everything:

**"Clevicular" and "Brandon Peters" are the same person.** The creator is **Clavicular** (note the spelling — like the *clavicle*, the collarbone, which is a "looksmaxxing" obsession). His real name is **Braden Peters** — close to the "Brandon Peters" you remembered. So you weren't looking at two case studies. You were looking at one person from two angles, which is itself a lesson: a personal brand is a *character* layered on top of a real human, and the gap between the two is where a lot of the psychology lives.

We'll use him as our primary case study — not as a hero, and honestly not as a villain either, but as a *specimen*. He is one of the cleanest examples in 2025-2026 of how the attention economy actually works, including its dark edges.

---

## The four lenses

We study every case and idea through four lenses. Keep these in your head like a toolkit:

1. **The Economic lens** — Attention is scarce and therefore valuable. Who is paying, with what, for whose attention? (Herbert Simon, the "attention economy.")
2. **The Psychological lens** — Why does a one-sided relationship feel real? (Parasocial bonds, identification, intermittent reward, pain points.)
3. **The Philosophical lens** — What *is* attention, and what do we owe it? (William James, Simone Weil.)
4. **The Ethical lens** — Where is the line between *serving* a need and *manufacturing* one? (Persuasive design, the looksmaxxing pipeline.)

A good analysis touches all four. If you only ever say "the algorithm did it," you're stuck on lens 1.

## A 6-week lesson plan

This is paced for ~3-4 hours per week. Each week has a **read**, a **watch/observe**, a **write**, and a **question to sit with**. The writing is the point — you don't understand attention until you can explain it in your own words.

### Week 1 — The hook: one case, four lenses
- **Read:** Lesson 01 (the Clavicular case study).
- **Observe:** Spend 20 minutes watching how *any* creator you like opens their videos. Write down the first 10 seconds beat-by-beat.
- **Write:** A one-page "field report" on the Clavicular case using the four lenses above.
- **Sit with:** Did this person *create* an insecurity, or *find* one that was already there? Does the answer change how you judge him?

### Week 2 — The attention economy
- **Read:** Lesson 02, sections on Herbert Simon and Tristan Harris.
- **Observe:** Track your own screen time for the week. Note the *moment* you reach for your phone — what feeling precedes it?
- **Write:** "What is my attention worth, and who is currently buying it?" Estimate the dollar value of your daily attention to the platforms you use.
- **Sit with:** If attention is a currency, are you spending it or is it being taken?

### Week 3 — Parasocial bonds
- **Read:** Lesson 02, parasocial section + the YourTango and NetPsychology pieces in Sources.
- **Observe:** Pick one creator you feel "close" to. List every reason the closeness feels real (they say your name? reply to comments? share struggles?).
- **Write:** Map the *mechanics* of that intimacy. Which are genuine and which are designed?
- **Sit with:** Is a parasocial relationship a kind of friendship, a kind of consumption, or something genuinely new?

### Week 4 — Pain points and persuasion
- **Read:** Lesson 02, the pain-point + persuasive-design sections, plus the BPS "algorithmic mirror" piece.
- **Observe:** Find three ads or creator pitches. For each, name the exact pain point and the promised relief.
- **Write:** Build a "pain-point map" for the looksmaxxing audience: the surface desire, the deeper fear, the identity on offer.
- **Sit with:** Where is the line between marketing and manipulation? Can you state it as a rule, not a vibe?

### Week 5 — The philosophy of attention
- **Read:** Lesson 02, James & Weil sections, plus the LitHub essay on Weil.
- **Observe:** Do one 30-minute session of *Weil-style attention* — read or watch something hard with zero second screen. Notice the resistance.
- **Write:** Compare James's "taking possession by the mind" with Weil's "negative effort" of receptive waiting. Which describes scrolling? Which describes learning?
- **Sit with:** Weil said attention is "the rarest and purest form of generosity." If that's true, what does an infinite feed do to your capacity for generosity?

### Week 6 — Synthesis: build your own theory
- **Read:** Re-skim both lessons and your own field reports.
- **Write:** A 1,500-word essay: *"How a stranger becomes someone you trust."* Use Clavicular as the case, all four lenses as the structure, and end with one ethical principle you'd defend.
- **Make:** Turn the essay into something public — a blog post, a video script, a thread. Teaching it is the final test of understanding.
- **Sit with:** Now that you can see the machinery, do you want to *use* it, *resist* it, or *redesign* it?

## How to use this notebook
- Add a `notes/` folder and keep your weekly field reports there. Date them.
- When you find a new case (another creator, another rise-to-fame story), add a lesson file like `03-case-[name].md` and run it through the four lenses.
- Disagree with these notes in writing. The point is *your* model, not mine.
